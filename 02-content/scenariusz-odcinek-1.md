## #1/10 — BUSY BEE vs KOOL MOE DEE (0:25–1:50)

`[Badge #1/10. Zdjęcia: Harlem World club, 1981]`

Every beef on this list exists because of one night in December 1981. Busy Bee
Starski was the king of party rap — call-and-response, crowd-pleasing, simple.
At a Christmas battle at Harlem World, he did his usual routine. Then Kool Moe
Dee of the Treacherous Three grabbed the mic — and instead of rocking the
crowd, he attacked Busy Bee directly. By name. Line after line, dismantling
his style as childish. Nobody had ever done that before. The recording of that
night spread on cassette tapes across New York like a virus. **[SHORT]** In one
night, rap stopped being just a party trick and became a competitive sport.
Every diss track you've ever heard traces back to that stage. But the first
beef that actually sold records? That was started by a teenage girl.

## #2/10 — THE ROXANNE WARS (1:50–3:15)

`[Badge #2/10. Okładka "Roxanne, Roxanne" UTFO, zdjęcie Roxanne Shanté]`

In 1984, the group UTFO made "Roxanne, Roxanne" — a song complaining about a
fictional girl who rejected them. A 14-year-old from Queensbridge named Lolita
Shanté Gooden heard it and recorded an answer in one take: "Roxanne's Revenge",
rapping AS Roxanne, destroying every member of the group. It sold around
250,000 copies. UTFO responded by hiring their own "Real Roxanne". And then
things got out of control: over the next year, somewhere between 30 and 100
different Roxanne answer records were released. Roxanne's parents made a
record. Roxanne's doctor made a record. **[SHORT]** The Roxanne Wars proved
beef SELLS — and made Roxanne Shanté the first battle queen of hip-hop. The
next war wasn't about a girl, though. It was about geography.

## #3/10 — THE BRIDGE WARS: KRS-ONE vs MC SHAN (3:15–4:45)

`[Badge #3/10. Mapa: South Bronx vs Queensbridge, okładki "The Bridge" / "The Bridge Is Over"]`

1986. MC Shan and producer Marley Marl of the Juice Crew release "The Bridge" —
a tribute to their home, the Queensbridge projects. Across the river, a young
KRS-One hears it as a claim that hip-hop STARTED in Queensbridge. Which, to a
Bronx native, is blasphemy — hip-hop was born in the Bronx, 1973, and KRS took
it personally. His responses, "South Bronx" and then "The Bridge Is Over",
were so devastating that they essentially defined how you win a beef: not by
being angrier, but by being catchier. "The Bridge Is Over" is still played in
clubs today. Shan's career never fully recovered. Lesson number one of rap
warfare: **[SHORT]** the winner is whoever makes the diss you can dance to.
For a decade, that's all beef was — records and battles. Then it turned deadly.

## #4/10 — TUPAC vs THE NOTORIOUS B.I.G. (4:45–6:30)

`[Badge #4/10. Ton poważnieje, muzyka ścisza. Zdjęcia archiwalne, oś czasu 1994–1997]`

The biggest beef in hip-hop history — and the one with no winners. Tupac
Shakur and Biggie Smalls started as friends. In November 1994, Pac was shot
five times in the lobby of a New York studio — and he became convinced Biggie
and Bad Boy Records knew about it. From there, a personal conflict became a
coastal war: Death Row Records in LA versus Bad Boy in New York, West versus
East, with the media pouring gasoline on it. Pac's "Hit 'Em Up" remains the
most vicious diss ever recorded. In September 1996, Tupac was murdered in Las
Vegas. Six months later, in March 1997, Biggie was murdered in Los Angeles. He
was 24. Pac was 25. I'll do a full lecture on this one — the timeline, the
theories, the investigations — because it deserves more than two minutes.
Here, the point is this: after 1997, hip-hop learned where the line is. And
the next great war deliberately stayed on wax.

## #5/10 — JAY-Z vs NAS (6:30–8:10)

`[Badge #5/10. Summer Jam 2001, okładki "Takeover"/"Ether"]`

New York, 2001. The throne Biggie left empty had two claimants: Jay-Z and Nas.
At Summer Jam 2001, Jay premiered "Takeover" — a cold, factual demolition of
Nas's career: one classic album, then nothing, "a one-hot-album-every-ten-year
average." Nas was, at the time, considered washed. Then he answered with
"Ether" — pure, concentrated disrespect, so effective that "to ether someone"
is now a verb in the English language. **[SHORT]** Jay responded with "Super
Ugly" — and went so far below the belt that his own mother made him apologize
on the radio. Who won? Polls at the time said Nas. History says: both. The
beef revived Nas's career and gave Jay his hunger back — and in 2005 they
ended it on stage together, and Nas signed to Jay's Def Jam. The perfect beef:
two legends, zero funerals, three classic records.

`[MID-ROLL: zawieszenie przed przerwą]`

And the next one? The next one wasn't a war of poets. It was a hostile
corporate takeover disguised as a beef.

## #6/10 — 50 CENT vs JA RULE (8:10–9:40)

`[Badge #6/10. Okładki, wykres sprzedaży Murder Inc. 2002→2005]`

Early 2000s: Ja Rule is one of the biggest stars on Earth — melodic hooks,
duets, pop charts. 50 Cent was a Queens mixtape rapper with a genuine street
conflict with Ja's circle, going back to a robbery and a stabbing. When 50
signed with Eminem and Dr. Dre in 2002, he weaponized that history: not one
diss, but a total campaign — "Wanksta", "Back Down", relentless mockery of Ja
Rule's singing. And it worked like nothing before or since: Ja Rule didn't
just lose a beef, his entire label Murder Inc. collapsed commercially within
three years. **[SHORT]** 50 proved a beef can be a marketing strategy — a
planned demolition of a competitor to take his market share. Cold-blooded,
and it built an empire. Speaking of 50's boss — you can't make a beef list
without the man nobody ever beat.

## #7/10 — EMINEM vs EVERYBODY (9:40–11:00)

`[Badge #7/10. Szybki montaż: Benzino, Ja Rule, Nick Cannon, MGK]`

Eminem doesn't have A beef. Eminem has a career-long policy. The Source
magazine and Benzino called him a culture thief — Em responded with "Nail in
the Coffin" and the magazine's credibility never recovered. Ja Rule went at
him during the 50 Cent war and got "Hailie's Revenge". But the modern classic
is Machine Gun Kelly: after years of tension over a tweet about Em's daughter,
MGK dropped "Rap Devil" in 2018 — genuinely good, by the way. Eminem answered
in days with "Killshot", which broke YouTube records at the time. **[SHORT]**
MGK then… switched genres entirely and became a pop-punk artist. When your
opponent leaves the entire sport, that's a technical knockout. But no diss on
this list — none — ended a career faster than number eight.

## #8/10 — DRAKE vs MEEK MILL (11:00–12:20)

`[Badge #8/10. Twitter screenshoty 2015, memy "Back to Back"]`

Summer 2015. Meek Mill tweets that Drake doesn't write his own raps and has a
ghostwriter — in hip-hop, still a capital offense. The internet waits for war.
Drake drops "Charged Up"... then, before Meek even answers, "Back to Back" —
the first diss track in history nominated for a Grammy, and more importantly,
an instant meme factory. Meek's response, "Wanna Know", came four days too
late and landed flat. That was it. Two songs, one week, career damage that
took Meek years to repair. **[SHORT]** Lesson: in the social media era, a beef
isn't won with bars alone — it's won with memes and speed. But Drake's story
doesn't end there. Because the ghostwriting question never went away.

## #9/10 — PUSHA T vs DRAKE (12:20–13:40)

`[Badge #9/10. Okładka "The Story of Adidon"]`

2018. A decade of subliminal shots between Drake and Pusha T finally
detonates. Pusha raps about Drake's ghostwriters on "Infrared"; Drake responds
within hours with "Duppy Freestyle" — confident, mocking. And then Pusha T
drops "The Story of Adidon" — and changes the rules of engagement forever. He
reveals to the world that Drake has a secret son. He uses a real photo of
Drake in blackface as the cover art. No jokes, no punchlines — a surgical
strike built on opposition research. Drake never responded. J Prince, his
mentor, publicly said the response Drake had recorded was too damaging to ever
release. **[SHORT]** Pusha won on points nobody knew were on the scoreboard —
and taught every rapper that receipts beat rhymes. Which brings us to the main
event. The biggest beef of the modern era.

## #10/10 — KENDRICK LAMAR vs DRAKE (13:40–15:20)

`[Badge #10/10. Oś czasu marzec–czerwiec 2024, rekordy streamingowe]`

Kendrick and Drake circled each other for over a decade — "Control" in 2013,
years of subliminals. In March 2024, on "Like That", Kendrick ends the "big
three" talk: "it's just big ME". What follows is the fastest, highest-stakes
exchange in beef history: Drake fires "Push Ups" and "Taylor Made", Kendrick
answers with "Euphoria", "6:16 in LA", then — within 36 hours — "Meet the
Grahams" and "Not Like Us". Accusations from both sides get genuinely dark.
And "Not Like Us" becomes something no diss track has ever been: a number one
global hit, a record-breaking streaming phenomenon, a Grammy Record of the
Year winner, and the centerpiece of a Super Bowl halftime show — with the
target's name censored in the broadcast. **[SHORT]** Fifty years after
Kool Moe Dee grabbed that mic in Harlem, a battle rap performance headlined
the biggest stage in American entertainment. The sport Busy Bee lost that
night became the sport everybody watches.

## FINAL (15:20–16:00)

So what did we learn? Beef is hip-hop's engine — it made careers, ended
careers, and twice it ended lives. The winners were never the angriest.
They were the catchiest, the fastest, and the ones with receipts.
